Grossman & Stiglitz

The paradox that pays for research.
The work that changed it
1980
On the Impossibility of Informationally Efficient Markets
Grossman & Stiglitz
Why it matters
If prices reflected all information, nobody would be paid to gather it — so nobody would. Prices must stay imperfect enough to reward the research that improves them.
Key work
1980
Grossman & Stiglitz — On the Impossibility of Informationally Efficient Markets
2015

Core ideas
Information is costly; someone has to be paid to collect it
Perfect efficiency is self-defeating
Excess return is the wage of research, and the wage is competitive
The takeawayEfficient enough to be hard. Inefficient enough to be worth it.
Where the edge is
Your edge is the wage for information others found too expensive to gather. Ask what it costs to know this — and why the crowd will not pay it.

The argument, dated.

Five papers, thirty-four years. Read left to right and the case for research makes itself.
1970
Fama
The claim
Prices already reflect what is known. Beating the market is luck.
Efficient Capital Markets: A Review of Theory and Empirical Work
1979
Kahneman & Tversky
The counterparty
But people are not the calculators the claim assumes. Losing hurts more than winning feels good — and the mistake repeats.
Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk
1980
Grossman & Stiglitz
Why it must exist
If information costs money, prices cannot be perfect: somebody has to be paid to make them right.
On the Impossibility of Informationally Efficient Markets
1997
Shleifer & Vishny
Why it persists
Seeing the mispricing is not holding the trade. Capital, risk and horizon run out first.
The Limits of Arbitrage
2004
Lo

Edges get crowded and decay, the environment shifts, new ones appear. Nothing stays put.
The Adaptive Markets Hypothesis
A return to research has to exist. It has a source. It persists. And it keeps moving.

The evidence, dated.

Five results, twelve years. Edges are real, they decay — and whether they were ever real is still being fought over.
2011
Khandani & Lo
Crowding
August 2007: quant books that shared a strategy unwound together in three days. An ecological shock, reconstructed.
What Happened to the Quants in August 2007? Evidence from Factors and Transactions Data
2016
Daniel & Moskowitz
Cycles
Momentum crashes in sharp rebounds. A strategy works, gets crowded, breaks — and comes back.
Momentum Crashes
2016
McLean & Pontiff
Decay
Anomaly returns fall about 26% out of sample and about 58% once the paper is published.
Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability?
2016
Harvey, Liu & Zhu
False positives
Hundreds of factors published; most claimed findings are false once you correct for the number of tests.
… and the Cross-Section of Expected Returns
2023
Jensen, Kelly & Pedersen
The rebuttal
Model the factors jointly and 82% replicate, in 13 themes across 93 countries.
Is There a Replication Crisis in Finance?
Real. Decaying. And still contested.
What it means for us
Nobody will settle the factor fight for you. Deciding which edges are real, and for how long, is the job — and it never finishes.

Theory
Who
Its role inside the frame

Where the maladaptive heuristics come from — shortcuts that worked in another environment.

Costly information
Grossman & Stiglitz, 1980

Reflexivity
Soros, 1987
Feedback between participants and prices — part of what changes the environment.
Each keeps its job. The frame is what lets them stop contradicting each other.

Why research gets paid.

01
It has to exist.
Grossman & Stiglitz, 1980 — if information is costly, someone is paid to gather it.
02
It has a source.
Behavioural finance — heuristics that worked in another environment keep firing in this one.
03
It persists.
Limits to arbitrage — capital, risk and horizon constraints on the people who see it.
04
It keeps moving.
Adaptive markets — edges crowd and decay, the environment shifts, new ones appear.
Said before anyone else does
The frame is hard to falsify.
So make it operational: measure crowding, capacity, flows and the participant mix.
Grossman–Stiglitz is the stronger claim.
Use it for why the return to research cannot go to zero; use Lo for how it moves.
Berk & Green cut the other way.
‘The return exists’ is not ‘the client gets it’ — capital chases skill until the net return is gone. Say which claim you are making.
The edge is in the process, not in any single signal.
What this Bootcamp builds
A research process you can rerun on the next idea, not a fixed strategy.

Act VII is unwritten.

Four things the arc has not answered. Each one is where the next edge is most likely to come from.
01
Regimes you can see coming
We name regimes confidently after the fact. Forecasting the turn in time to act on it is the difference between surviving a shift and explaining one.
Who is on it
Hamilton, 1989 · Ang & Timmermann, 2012 · the regime-switching and jump-model literature
Where it lands here
Session 03: features that carry regime information.
02
A census of the crowd
Who else holds this, with how much capital, on what horizon? That data is thin, late or proprietary — so crowding gets measured after it hurts.
Who is on it
Khandani & Lo, 2011 · flow and positioning research
Where it lands here
Session 05: capacity, costs and position limits.
03
Learning that generalises
Machine learning fits the past extremely well. The binding constraint is economic structure and sample size, not compute.
Who is on it
Gu, Kelly & Xiu, 2020 · Arnott, Harvey & Markowitz, 2019 · Jensen, Kelly & Pedersen, 2023
Where it lands here
Session 04: the backtest protocol, written before the test.
04
Research that runs itself
If edges decay, the asset is the pipeline: data, features, tests and attribution, re-runnable on tomorrow’s idea. Automate the process, not the signal.
Who is on it
Guo, Wang, Ni & Shum, 2022 — Quant 4.0 · knowledge-driven AI
Where it lands here
Every session: the strategy repository you started in session 01.
Six acts are written. The seventh is the work you do in the next four sessions.

Five sources of edge.

An idea without a named source of edge is a pattern. Say which of these it draws on — and why that door is still open — before you test anything.
01
Risk premia
You are paid to hold a risk others refuse to bear.
Ask
What risk am I carrying — and who is paying me for it?
Sharpe · Ross · Fama & French
02
Behavioural
People err in the same direction, again and again.
Ask
Which bias makes the counterparty sell to me?
Kahneman & Tversky · Thaler · Shiller
03
Limits to arbitrage
The people who know better cannot act on it.
Ask
Who cannot take the other side — and why not?
Shleifer & Vishny · Amihud
04
Information
You measure something the price has not absorbed.
Ask
What do I see that the price does not — and why me?
Grossman & Stiglitz · Fama · Gu, Kelly & Xiu
05
Implementation
Same signal — more of it kept after costs.
Ask
Can I capture a known edge better than the crowd does?
Markowitz · Grinold & Kahn · Paleologo
Where an edge comes from · idea generation
57

Where ideas come from.

Six places to look. Each comes with the way it fools you.
01
Published anomalies
The factor zoo: hundreds of documented patterns, most with a paper behind them.
The trap
Most are noise. Prefer ideas with a mechanism, replicated out of sample.
02
Market structure
Index rebalances, option expiries, month-end flows, forced sellers.
The trap
Crowded and small. Check capacity before you fall in love.
03
Behavioural patterns
Under-reaction to news, over-reaction to stories, the disposition effect.
The trap
Name the bias and who holds it — or it is a story too.
04
Frictions
Illiquidity, funding constraints, short-sale limits.
The trap
The premium is real. The cost of collecting it is what you are testing.
05
Your own data
Something you can measure that others do not — or measure better.
The trap
A new dataset is a hypothesis generator, not a hypothesis.
06
Combining known premia
Value with momentum. Trend with liquidity. Carry with trend.
The trap
Diversification is edge only if the pieces fail at different times.
Where an edge comes from · before any backtest
58

Seven questions before any backtest.

01
Why does this edge exist?
Grossman & Stiglitz · Fama
02
Who is on the other side, and why do they keep losing?
Kahneman & Tversky · Shleifer & Vishny
03
Is it a risk premium or a mispricing, and which one is the claim about?
Ross · Fama & French
04
How many independent bets a year does it give?
Grinold & Kahn
05
Does it survive costs, and how much capital can it hold?
Amihud · Paleologo
06
When does it fail, and for how long?
Lo · Shiller
07
How many ideas were tried before this one?
Harvey, Liu & Zhu · McLean & Pontiff
If the seven answers do not fit in one paragraph, it is not a hypothesis yet.
Where an edge comes from · anatomy
59

What a good idea looks like.

A belief is a sentence. A hypothesis names its edge, its counterparty, its test — and what would make you drop it.
The claim
One sentence, present tense, about a measurable relationship.
Liquid assets in an uptrend keep outperforming for weeks, not days.
The source of edge
Which of the five it draws on — and why that door should still be open.
Behavioural under-reaction, collected with an implementation edge.
The other side
Who sells to you, and what keeps them doing it.
Cap-weighted holders who rebalance on size, not on trend.
The test
The measurement that settles it, chosen before you look.
Rank by trend, weight by liquidity, hold one month, net of costs.
The kill switch
The result that makes you abandon the idea instead of tuning it.
No excess return over the equal-weight control, net of costs, in two of three sub-periods.
Discussion

Which one would you argue with?

Whose paper does your belief contradict — and are you comfortable with that?
If edges decay, what is the shelf life of the strategy you want to build?
What evidence would actually change your mind before you spend four months on it?
